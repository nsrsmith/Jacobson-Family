# Jacobson Sisters' Norway — how to read this page

This folder holds a family-history page about **Gertrude Betty Jacobson (1921–2015)** and her sisters — Violette, Lucille, Maxine, Opal — of Radcliffe, Iowa, and where their family came from before Iowa.

All four of Gertrude's grandparents were born in Norway, and all four lines come from the **same stretch of coast**: the fjords around Etne, Fjelberg and Ølen, in the district called **Sunnhordland**, south of Bergen. The page follows every one of them home — **62 people, eight generations, back to 1679** — and shows the farm or parish where each was born.

Everything you need is the single file **`index.html`**.

**Double-click `index.html`.** It opens in whatever web browser you already use. There is nothing to install, no account, no password.

## Finding your own line

This is the part most people want.

1. Scroll to **The lineage, four branches**.
2. Gertrude is at the top, with her parents underneath.
3. Click **"Open every generation"** to unfold the whole thing at once. (**"Collapse to grandparents"** puts it back.)
4. Below that, the tree splits into **four branches** — one for each of Gertrude's grandparents:

   - **The Jacobson line** — Olin's father, John Jacob Jacobson, born at Etne in 1847
   - **The Høiland line** — Olin's mother, Lovise, from the Høiland farm in Etne
   - **The Swenson / Arvik line** — Olive's father, Newton, from Arnevik in Fjelberg
   - **The Tollefsen / Aske line** — Olive's mother, Martha, from the Aske and Sæbø farms

Find the branch you belong to and follow it down. Each step down is one generation further back.

**Between the two** sits **The seven Jacobson children** — Olin and Olive's children in birth order, Gertrude fourth. The twins born in November 1924 came eleven days before their mother died, and neither lived past the following spring.

**On the maps:** hover over a pin to see who was born there. **Click** a pin and the page jumps to those people in the tree. The coloured dot on anyone's card works the same way in reverse.

## Reading a person's card

Every person has a card. It looks busier than it is. Here is what each part means.

**The line at the top** tells you how they are related to Gertrude — *"2× great-grandmother · father · mother · mother"* means: go from Gertrude to her father, then his mother, then her mother.

**The name** is a link. Click it to open that person in the Ancestry tree.

**The coloured badge** is the important one. It says how sure we are:

| Badge | What it means |
|---|---|
| **PROVEN IN THE RECORDS** | An actual Norwegian church or census record names this person and their parents. Solid. |
| **PROBABLE** | The records fit and nothing contradicts it, but no single document says it outright. |
| **NOT YET VERIFIED** | The name is in the family tree, but no record has been found yet. |
| **TREE IS WRONG HERE** | A record actually contradicts what the tree says. Read the note. |

**"What the Norwegian records say"** — click to open. This is the researched account: what the parish registers and censuses actually show about this person. If a line appears in **orange beginning "Correction:"**, that is a place where the Ancestry tree and the records disagree, with the records winning.

**The underlined links** below the note go to the actual record on Digitalarkivet, the Norwegian National Archives site. They are permanent.

**"Facts from the tree"** — click to open. This is the raw Ancestry entry, shown separately and unedited, so you can always see the difference between *what the tree says* and *what the records say*.

## Norwegian names — read this before anything confuses you

This trips up nearly everyone, and once it clicks the whole page gets easier.

**Nobody in Norway had a surname the way we do.** A person's second name told you their *father's* name:

- **-sen / -son** = "son of" — *Ole **Knudsen*** is Ole, son of Knud
- **-datter / -dotter** = "daughter of" — *Lucie **Thorsdatter*** is Lucie, daughter of Thor

So the "last name" **changes every single generation**, and brothers and sisters have different ones. Knud Olsen's son is Ole Knudsen; Ole Knudsen's son is Knud Olsen again. It alternates.

**The third name is the farm, not the family.** *Ole Knudsen **Høiland*** means Ole who lives at the Høiland farm. If he moved farms, that name changed too. It is an address, not a surname.

**American names were invented at the docks.** *Knud Ingebrigt Gudmundsen* of Arnevik became **Newton Knute Engebrit Swenson** in Iowa. *Johannes* became **John**. *Lovise* became **Louise**. Same people.

**One more:** the same first names repeat relentlessly, because children were named for grandparents in a fixed order. When a baby died, the next one born often got the same name. That is why you will see two sons called Michel, or three called Ole.

## A few Norwegian words you'll meet

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **gaardbruger / gårdbruker** | Farmer |
| **selveier** | Owner-occupier — owns his land outright |
| **husmann** (husmand) | Cottar — rents a small plot under a bigger farm. Landless. |
| **inderst / inderstpige** | A lodger; someone with no land and no house of their own |
| **føderåd / vilkår** | A written pension: an old farmer hands the farm to his heir in exchange for housing and food for life |
| **uægte** | Born outside marriage |
| **prestegjeld / sokn** | Parish / sub-parish — the unit church records are kept in |
| **tæring** | Consumption — tuberculosis |
| **enke / enkemand** | Widow / widower |

## Checking a record yourself

Anyone can do this; it is free and needs no account.

1. Click any underlined source link on a person's card.
2. It opens **Digitalarkivet** (the Norwegian National Archives) at that exact record.
3. Look for **"See scanned version"** — that shows you the photographed page from the original parish book, in the priest's own handwriting.

The handwriting is hard, but the **dates and the names are usually legible**, and it is a genuine thing to see: the actual page where an ancestor was written into the world.

## Technical notes

Everything is in `index.html`. It pulls in only two things from the internet: the D3 charting library and three Google fonts. Without a connection the maps won't draw, but all the text and the tree still work.
