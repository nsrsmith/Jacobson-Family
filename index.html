Sunnhordland, the home coast

All four lines come from one district. A short orientation to the place, its parishes, and why people left it.

Sunnhordland — "southern Hordaland" — is the stretch of coast south of Bergen where the Hardangerfjord opens to the sea. It is a landscape of islands and side-fjords: Stord, Bømlo, Tysnes and Halsnøy offshore; Etnefjorden, Åkrafjorden, Skånevikfjorden and Ølsfjorden cutting inland toward mountains, with the Folgefonna glacier in the east. About half the land lies above 300 metres, so people have always lived on the strips of flat ground at the fjord heads and along the shores — exactly where the family's farms sit. Today the district takes in seven municipalities (Sveio, Etne, Stord, Bømlo, Fitjar, Kvinnherad and Tysnes), around 59,000 people, with Leirvik on Stord as its centre, and a dialect of its own, Sunnhordlandsmål.

In the family's century the map was drawn differently. Everyone on the Sunnhordland pins lived in Søndre Bergenhus amt (the county), under the Sunnhordland sorenskriveri (the court district whose deed and probate books hold their land transfers and estates), inside a handful of prestegjeld — clerical parishes — each with its own church book. Those parishes are the addresses on every pin above.

The district's oldest history runs straight through the family's ground. Stødle in Etne was the seat of Erling Skakke, the twelfth-century jarl whose son Magnus Erlingsson was the first Norwegian king to be crowned, in 1163 or 1164. The stone church at Stødle, built about 1160 and probably as Erling's private chapel, is the church where Lovise Olsdatter was baptised on 24 August 1862 — seven hundred years on. To buy the archbishop's support for the coronation, Erling also founded Halsnøy Abbey, an Augustinian house on Halsnøy, a short walk from the Sæbø farm where the Tollefsen line was living by 1705. The abbey was dissolved in 1536; its ruins have been conserved by the Sunnhordland Museum since 1956.

Why they left

A good farm went whole to the eldest son under åsetesrett; the other children were paid out in cash and had to find a croft, a trade, or a ship. The 1865 census at Høiland shows the pattern in one household — an owner, a retired father on a føderåd pension, and two grown sons working as day labourers with no land and no prospect of marriage. Norwegian emigration surged from 1866, and Sunnhordland's natural port was Bergen, where the police kept the protocol that records Lovise's departure in 1881 on a ticket someone in America had already paid for.

    What the records prove

    On 4 September 2026 the Norwegian side of the tree was checked against the National Archives' transcribed censuses (1801, 1865, 1875, 1900, 1910) and church books for Fjelberg, Etne, Skånevik and Vikedal. Every person card above now carries a verdict and links to the records.

    What held up

    1. The Høiland line is proven end to end. Ole Knudsen's 1831 baptism names Knud Olsen and Lucie Thorsdatter; his 1856 wedding at Vikedal names Knud Olsen of Høiland and Endre Endresen of Nedre Helgevold; Thordis's 1835 baptism at Sandeid names Endre Endresen and Anna Olsdatter of Tronsvold. Knud and Lucie's own 1830 wedding at Skånevik names Ole Olsen Høvland and Thor Olsen Hegland as their fathers — two names the tree did not have.
    2. The Aske–Sæbø line is proven back to 1801, and consistent to 1730. Tollef's baptism (1827), his parents' wedding (1824), Tollev's baptism (1801) and the 1801 census at Aske line up exactly; Guri's baptism (1792) and the 1801 census at Sæbø tie her to Tosten and Barbro, with Halvor Simonsen (72) in the next household.
    3. The Swenson line is proven to 1801. Newton is Knud Ingebrigt, baptised 21 Jul 1850 at Arnevik; his parents' 1844 wedding names Svend Larssen and Anfind Ingebrigtsen; the 1801 census shows Anfind, aged 4, in Ingebrigt Steensen's cottage at Utbjoa, with Steen Halvorsen and Anna in the household next door.
    4. John Jacob Jacobson found. The Grundy County marriage (1883) and Iowa death (1914) records name his parents as Jacob Jacobson and Martha "Shold"; the Etne register has Johannes, born 13 Aug 1847, out of wedlock, to Martha Thorsdatter of Skjold and Jacob Johannesen of Hovland — and in 1865 he is a 19-year-old farm servant at Øien nordre, Etne. He was from Lovise's own parish.
    5. Two new generations. Knud Jonsen of Aske's father, Jon Sjursen (b. c. 1720), was living with him in 1801; Lyder Iversen of Dale's mother, Inger Christensdatter (b. c. 1736), was living with him the same year.

    What the tree gets wrong

    1. The Vefsn branch is a mis-merge. The 1801 census shows Sven Larsen, aged 9, at Aarvik in Fjelberg with parents Lars (44) and Karen (39). The tree's Lars Svendsen of Kvalfors, Vefsn — 1,000 km north, died there in 1830 — is a different man with the same name and birth year. The five Vefsn people and their pins should be detached.
    2. Anna Knudsdatter was born at Aske, not to "Knud Strand". Her 1825 wedding gives her age as 22 and her birthplace as Aske; her father was Knud Jonsen. That makes her Tollev Knudsen's sister — so Tollef and Magnilde, who married in 1847, were first cousins.
    3. Iver Lydersen was born in 1802, not 1778. Baptised 4 Aug 1802, married at 24 in 1825, widowed c. 1828, remarried 1829. His parents were Lyder Iversen and Marta Persdatter of Dale.
    4. Dates to fix: Guri Tostensdatter baptised Feb 1792 (not 1794); Helga Anfindsdatter baptised Mar 1823 (not born Apr 1824); Thordis born 2 Feb 1835 (not 1834) and buried 8 Oct 1914 (not "after 1910"); Lucie died at Høiland in Oct 1895.
    5. Wrong records attached: Tollef Tollefsen's "baptism at Rendal, Hedmark 1826" and "arrival New York 1847" belong to someone else — he married at Fjelberg in Sept 1847 and was farming Aske in 1865. Martha's "1863 arrival" is wrong (she is at Aske, aged 11, in 1865); Helga's "1855 arrival" is wrong (a child of hers was baptised at Arnevik in Aug 1856). Newton's "Haugesund" birthplace should be Arnevik. Anna Olsdotter's Kragerø birthplace is unsupported.
